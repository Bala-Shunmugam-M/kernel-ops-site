Inspection planning for maintenance teams

Know which machine to inspect next, and be able to check why

KERNEL-OPS reads the telemetry and work orders you already have and proposes which assets need an inspection this week, within the crew capacity you set. Every proposal comes with the evidence it was based on, limited to what was known at the moment it was made.

Run a shadow pilot See the evidence so far

What it does

Proposes inspection jobs

Scores each enrolled asset every day and records the inspections it would ask for, never more per week than your crew can take. Repeat alerts on one machine join its open job instead of piling up.

Uses only what was known at the time

A reading that arrived late, or a diagnosis typed in days after a job, is never treated as known earlier. Any past decision can be rebuilt exactly as it was made.

Shows its evidence

Each alert opens an evidence pack: the readings and maintenance history before the decision, what has been learned since, and what is missing. A morning brief lists what ran, what needs review and what is blocked.

Keeps your team in charge

Your maintenance lead records each outcome: fault confirmed, no fault found, or not looked at. Nothing is dispatched automatically, and an optional AI assistant only drafts notes for a person to judge. It is off unless you set a spending cap.

The shadow pilot

The first step with any site is a shadow pilot on one asset class: the system watches and records, and changes nothing.

Shadow boundary

Nothing is dispatched. No crew is sent, no work order is created in your maintenance system, and your maintenance plan runs exactly as it does today.

Before day one

One intake conversation with your maintenance lead, and a small export of a few months of sensor data, work orders and, if you keep them, alarm or fault-code logs. A readiness check refuses to start while any decision you need to make is still blank.

Every day

Automatic. New data is loaded, each enrolled asset is scored, and proposed inspection jobs are recorded within your weekly crew capacity.

Every week

A short review with your maintenance lead, recording what happened to each proposed job. How long it takes depends on the capacity you set; we agree it up front.

At the end

A report setting every proposal against what happened to that asset, with every assumption the pilot rested on.

How a pilot is judged

A

Did it run reliably?

Every day processed or recovered, nothing lost, delays explained.

B

Did it fit your routine?

Every proposal reviewed in time, and your lead finds the queue workable.

C

Does it predict failures?

Answered only once enough real failures have happened and been followed up. Not promised by the pilot.

The evidence so far

We test the model the way we would want it tested at your site. Each test is written down before its data is scored, run once, and has to beat chance baselines, such as the same failures moved to random times. On public data from real machines, it has not yet shown that it predicts failures.

What this tells us

Warning signs differ between machines and over time, so a method fitted to one machine does not carry over to the next. The honest test is on your equipment, scored every day before the outcome is known, which is what a shadow pilot does.

What it also showed

Recorded events such as alarms and fault codes mattered more than the choice of model. That is why the pilot asks for your alarm logs, not only sensor readings.

What we need from a site

  • A maintenance owner

    The person who would decide whether an alert becomes a work order, and who signs off the findings.

  • One asset class

    Several similar machines, one ID per machine. If a machine appears under two IDs, we enroll the one you trust.

  • Telemetry, and when it arrives

    Signals, sampling interval, timezone, and how late data reaches you.

  • Alarm and fault-code logs

    With their times, if you keep them. They mattered most in our tests.

  • Work orders with their edit history

    So a diagnosis added later is never read as known earlier.

  • Capacity and three costs

    How many alert-driven inspections a week the crew could take, and the cost of downtime, of an inspection and of a false alarm.

Common questions

Does our data leave our site?

Only if you choose. The pilot runs on one machine with local storage, and you decide where that machine sits and what may leave it.

Will it change how we do maintenance?

Not during a shadow pilot. It records proposals; your crew and your maintenance system work exactly as before.

Do you use AI to make the decisions?

No. Scores and proposals come from a model trained on your data. An optional assistant can draft an inspection note from the evidence, labelled as a draft, and it cannot record outcomes or change any setting. It makes no paid calls unless you set a spending cap.

What if nothing fails during the pilot?

Then the pilot still answers whether it ran reliably and fitted your routine. Whether it predicts failures waits until enough real outcomes exist.
